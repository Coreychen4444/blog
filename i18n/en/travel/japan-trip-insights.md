I've been to Japan five times now, and some people might ask: why is Japan worth going back to again and again? Is it the stunning red leaves of Kyoto in late autumn, or the fireworks over the streets of Hakodate just before Christmas? For me, neither of those is what really draws me in. As an "observer" who likes to capture moments with a shutter, I'm more fascinated by the logic behind this country's scenery. What I want to share are some fragments of insight about three things: "people, things, cities."

## 1. Order and restraint: the "invisible contract" that keeps Japanese society running

Once you land in Japan, you're bound to run into a language barrier. Between limited English and our not knowing Japanese, communication doesn't always go smoothly. What's impressive, though, is the patience Japanese people show as listeners: even when they can't understand your words, they patiently pick up on your expressions and gestures and try to "piece together" what you mean, without showing the slightest hint of brushing you off or getting impatient. That kind of empathy across a language gap gives visitors a great sense of security and the dignity of being respected. Someone might ask: why not just use a translation app? My habit is to ask the other person first before using one, rather than shoving my phone right in their face. I think that's a basic form of respect too.

Beyond language, the way Japanese people give way to others is also worth praising. Letting passengers off the train before boarding, making room in elevators, queues that form on their own: these are already the baseline of the society. But what struck me most about giving way was how people "walk around" when someone is taking photos. I remember we were taking pictures on the observation deck at Kobe University that day. Anyone who's been there knows the deck is a flat, rectangular passage connecting a café and a reading room, and it's a public area. To get a "framed view" shot, the photographer has to stand at one end of the rectangle and shoot toward the other. We were completely absorbed, and wondering why such a stunning spot had no passersby getting in the way. Only when I turned around did I see that everyone walking through the passage had, without any coordination, gone along the outermost edge, that is, behind us, forming a curved detour. Why do I call it a "detour"? Because they could have simply walked straight through; they had no obligation to walk farther just because I was shooting, and the passage isn't small, either.

After that, I rethought the act of saying thanks after someone gives way. I realized that thanks shouldn't be a polite formality; it should come from genuine resonance. Because some acts of giving way aren't required at all. The other person has chosen to give up their own "convenience" to make your "experience" possible. When you respect others, you also receive more respect, and maybe that is what mutual respect really means. Perhaps the tea ceremony's ichigo ichie really is ingrained in Japanese people.

## 2. Empathetic design: the "silent care" hidden in tiny details

If you've traveled in Japan and paid attention, or even if you didn't make a point of it and just went about your usual daily habits, at some moment you'll be surprised: how can they think of everything so thoroughly and thoughtfully? Japanese design hides details you'd never expect everywhere, and it makes you feel the designers really used the products themselves. Whether it's a two-star or a five-star hotel, nothing that should be there is missing, and the rooms are just as neat and clean.

In restaurants, even at a budget fast-food chain like Yoshinoya, ordering a raw egg comes with two bowls as standard (one to crack the egg into, one to mix it with the rice or hold the shell). In unisex restrooms, there are signs explicitly asking men to sit down when urinating too, and an Otohime is provided to mask the sounds of using the toilet and protect privacy. In the hot-drinks section of convenience stores, the special packaging for plain hot water both insulates and doubles as a hand warmer. Then there's the notch on milk cartons (so visually impaired people can tell plain milk apart), the "three-step opening" of onigiri wrappers, Braille on canned drinks, and so on. The details are countless.

Beyond caring for individuals, Japan's care for particular groups also shows in how society's order runs.

In Japan, whether at a tourist spot, a station or an airport, you're sure to see group after group of students on school trips. What surprised me is that if you look carefully, these places often have dedicated "lanes" for school trips: stations have trains specifically marked "school trips only," and airports even have dedicated check-in counters. These special arrangements for students not only make large-scale group movement efficient, they also keep it from disturbing regular travelers. This kind of society-wide support for "the next generation growing up" is an extremely important part of caring for people, too.

What I want to say is that if you try putting yourself into a day in a Japanese person's life, slowing down and experiencing it, you'll feel the details and the care for people everywhere. Perhaps right when you're wondering "why isn't there such-and-such," you'll turn around and find what you need right in front of you. It's just that these design details are usually inconspicuous; you only notice they exist when you need them.

Speaking of details, I'd like to take the chance to correct a popular misconception about Japanese toilet paper. Many people complain that it's very thin and even tears easily. That's actually because smart toilets are extremely widespread in Japan; in restaurants and malls alike, you see them almost everywhere. Their washing functions are very well developed: men and women can choose different wash positions, water pressure and temperature, and the cleaning works extremely well. So, as I see it, the main purpose of Japanese toilet paper is simply to "dry off." In many newer restrooms, the toilet even has a drying function, and together with an automatic lid, you can go through the whole visit without your hands ever touching anything that might be dirty.

## 3. Extreme efficiency: a people-first network of "urban capillaries"

Because I play Cities: Skylines, the game gave me a reason to learn some urban-planning basics, so while traveling I naturally find myself thinking about the underlying logic of this.

Many people arriving in Tokyo for the first time are bound to get confused by its incredibly complex transit network. But once you take a local's perspective and really understand the logic of the whole system, you'll find it's actually an extremely efficient and convenient system, and the foundation that lets Tokyo support such a huge, high-density urban cluster.

First of all, what we usually call "Tokyo" is actually the Greater Tokyo Area (Tokyo plus three prefectures). The trains running on its rails fall mainly into four categories: JR, the Tokyo subway, private railways and the Shinkansen. Service types are further divided into Local, Rapid, Express, Limited Express and so on.

Tokyo's development philosophy is "railways first, then the city." Different railway companies compete in a free market to build lines and grab land. Once a line is built, they develop commercial centers and residential areas around the stations, raising land values to get residents to buy homes; then they add all kinds of everyday amenities along the line, quietly drawing residents into the "living sphere" the railway company has built. The money ultimately flows back to the railway company, and as this cycle repeats, the city keeps expanding. The satellite towns built this way are connected to central Tokyo by limited express trains, making for extremely efficient commuting. For example, getting from Yokohama to Tokyo Station takes less than 30 minutes.

It's precisely because of this model that Tokyo's transit coverage is astonishingly high, forming a "capillary" network that reaches deep into neighborhoods. You'll hardly find a residential area more than a 15-minute walk from a station. Add the track sharing and through-service arrangements the stations use, and you can take JR, the subway and even the Shinkansen from the same station.

Just imagine: no matter where you live in Tokyo, within a 15-minute walk from your door you can reach a multifunctional high-speed rail station that connects to the whole country.

You could say the station is the soul and center of the community. You'll notice that within 500 meters of every major station in Tokyo you'll always find the area's densest concentration of restaurants, clinics and convenience stores. On average there's a convenience store that meets your daily needs less than every 150 meters, so your whole living sphere is covered without relying on any private vehicle at all (not even an e-scooter). In this kind of "walkable" urban planning, Japanese people can enjoy a very high quality of life even without buying a car. And because people don't have to rely too heavily on food delivery and online shopping, brick-and-mortar shops are bound to thrive. That's also why Tokyo, despite not having many tall buildings, always looks lively from street to street.

## Closing: at the core of the order is respect for "people"

From the extreme restraint between people, to the silent care hidden in the design of things, to a city network that runs as precisely as capillaries.

When I pieced together the fragments from these three dimensions, I found that underneath all the logic of how Japanese society runs, everything actually points to one word: respect.

This respect is the awareness not to trouble others, the thoughtfulness that anticipates every inconvenience you might have, and above all the wisdom to make the most of limited space. It lets everyone (whether a local resident or a passing tourist, or even people with disabilities or visual impairments) live with decency and dignity in this high-density society.
